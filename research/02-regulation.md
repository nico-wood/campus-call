# Campus Call — regulation research memo

## Not legal advice

This is not legal advice. It is a student research memo for a hackathon. Nico should have a lawyer review it before any prize with real value or any real-money feature.

Campus Call is a free forecasting contest. People take positions, see a market probability, and earn points. The statutes quoted below use other words. Those words appear only inside quotations of the law or of a third party's own page. They should not appear in the product, the rules, or promo copy.

Research date: November 13, 2026. Primary pages were opened where noted. If a page failed, that is said in the section.

## What's safe now

Safe for the hackathon demo means all of the following at once:

- Every person gets the same virtual credits for free.
- Credits cannot be bought, sold, transferred, or redeemed. They have no cash value. They are a license to use the demo, not property.
- Nothing is paid to enter, to get more credits, or to improve a score.
- No sponsor product has to be bought, scanned, or signed up for.
- If there is a prize, it is awarded for forecasting skill: a published score (Brier score or points derived from it), not a drawing and not by cashing in a single position.
- The organizer does not take a cut of anything.
- Topics stay off education records, unpublished exams, a single student's result, a small class, a person's health, and anything that would reward a harmful or illegal act.
- The demo does not link to, refer people into, or imitate a real-money exchange.
- Copy does not say the University, the hackathon, or a sponsor runs or endorses the demo unless that person has agreed in writing.

On the face of the statutes opened below, that fact pattern is outside the core of California Penal Code sections 330 and 337a: section 330 is aimed at listed games and at a banking or percentage game played for money, checks, credit, or another representative of value; section 337a is aimed at pools and at recording or accepting a stake of value on a contest or on a contingent event. A free forecasting game with nothing staked is not that fact pattern.

It is not a court holding that the demo is legal. The California Attorney General's July 3, 2025 opinion (No. 23-1001) shows how fast the same idea becomes illegal once an entry fee is a stake on a sports result. See section 1.

Facts that pull the demo inside those prohibitions:

- An entry fee, or any payment to get credits or to keep a position.
- Credits that can be purchased. Business and Professions Code section 17539.1(b), in force for this hackathon, defines "direct consideration" as a coin, token, or other representation of value that may be purchased, or received through a bonus, and used to play.
- Credits that can be exchanged for a prize, cash, or a chance at a prize. The same section defines that as "indirect consideration," even if the credits were provided free.
- A prize paid from stakes, or a house percentage.
- A random drawing, including a drawing among people who hold a "winning" position.
- A market on a Cal Poly athletic contest aimed at student-athletes, or that asks them for team information. See section 6.
- Calling the product by the words those statutes use, in the interface or the rules. Labels do not decide a case, but they are evidence of what the operator thinks it is doing.

Penal Code section 336.9 does not make an online version safer. It is discussed in section 1. Do not use it as a design target.

## What needs organizer approval

Get a written yes from the hackathon organizers before any of these:

- A market on the hackathon itself: who wins, which team advances, what the judges pick, or anything a participant can influence.
- Using the hackathon's name, Cal Poly's name, or a sponsor's name as if they operate or endorse Campus Call.
- Announcing a prize, a leaderboard, or rules in an official Discord, email, or event channel.
- Any sponsor prize, sponsor credit grant, or sponsor-branded market.

Title 5, section 41301(b)(1)(D), as posted by Cal Poly OSRR, makes it a conduct offense to misrepresent oneself as an authorized agent of the University or one of its auxiliaries. A sloppy "official hackathon forecasting market" line can trip that even when no money moves.

Teams must not forecast their own outcome. Judges, organizers, and employees of an event being forecast must not take positions on that event. Put that in the rules and enforce it in the product.

## What needs a lawyer later

Stop and get a lawyer, and do not ship, before any of these:

- Any prize worth more than a trivial hackathon thank-you (a sticker, a printed certificate). Cash, gift cards, electronics, internship interviews, and sponsor swag of real value are in this bucket.
- Any referral, affiliate link, or "play for real" pointer to Kalshi, Polymarket, or any other real-money venue.
- Any sponsored market where credits, entries, or a better score come with a purchase, a signup, or a scan.
- Any expansion off the hackathon floor onto the public internet, into housing, or to people who are not in the room.
- Selling credits, even if they "cannot be cashed out." Manifold's own terms say that, and California's 2026 sweepstakes amendments describe purchased tokens as consideration. See sections 2 and 5.
- A chance prize of any kind.

This memo is not that legal review.

## 1. California Penal Code 330 and 337a

Sources opened: the official leginfo pages for [Penal Code 330](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=330.&lawCode=PEN), [Penal Code 337a](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=337a), and the leginfo text of [Penal Code 336.9](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=336.9) and [Penal Code 319](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=319). Section 330.2 was returned in full by leginfo search; it is short and is cited only for its limited scope.

**Section 330** prohibits dealing, playing, carrying on, opening, or conducting specified games (faro, monte, roulette, and the other games named in the section) "or any banking or percentage game played with cards, dice, or any device, for money, checks, credit, or other representative of value," and prohibits playing or staking value at those games. It is a misdemeanor. A forecasting app is not one of the games named in the sentence. This memo does not claim a forecasting screen is, or is not, a "banking or percentage game." No case opened for this memo held that. The quoted element that matters for design is "for money, checks, credit, or other representative of value." Free credits with no cash value and no resale are not, on this text, money or a representative of value. Credits someone can buy, or turn in for a prize, are the facts that make the "credit, or other representative of value" phrase arguable. Do not add a house percentage.

**Section 330.2** defines "thing of value" to include "any money, coin, currency, check, chip, allowance, token, credit, merchandise, property, or any representative of value." The section says that definition is for "Sections 330.1 to 330.5, inclusive" (device offenses). It is not the definition section for 330 or 337a. It is a warning about tokens, not a holding that hackathon points are illegal.

**Section 337a** is the closer statute. Except as provided in section 336.9, it punishes, including for a single instance (subdivision (e)), and "whether for gain, hire, reward, or gratuitously":

- Pool selling or bookmaking.
- Keeping any place with papers or a device "for the purpose of recording or registering any bet or bets ... upon the result ... of any trial ... or contest ... or upon the result ... of any lot, chance, casualty, unknown or contingent event whatsoever."
- Receiving "any money, thing or consideration of value" that is staked on such a result.
- Recording such a stake.
- Permitting a place to be used for those purposes.
- Laying, making, offering, or accepting such a stake "upon the result ... of any trial ... or contest ... of skill, speed or power of endurance."

A first offense can be county jail up to one year, or state prison, or a fine not to exceed five thousand dollars, or both. The word "gratuitously" means the operator does not have to profit. It does not say that a game with no stake of value is still covered. Subdivisions (a)(3) and (a)(6) are written in terms of a stake of value. Subdivisions (a)(1), (a)(2), and (a)(4) are written in terms of pools and of recording stakes. A demo that never takes value, never redeems credits, and never pools a prize out of stakes is outside that operative language. A lawyer could still argue about labels and about credits that function as value. That is why the facts in "What's safe now" have to stay true in the software, not only in a footer.

**Section 336.9** is the exception named in 337a. It does not legalize a private pool. It drops some in-person, not-for-profit participation on a lawful contest down to an infraction (a fine not to exceed two hundred fifty dollars). Subdivision (b) says that reduction "does not apply" to stakes "made online," or to pools with more than two thousand five hundred dollars at stake. An internet forecasting app does not get the lighter treatment. This memo does not describe how to fit any real-value pool under that section. Do not try.

**What stays outside, and what pulls the demo in.** Outside, on these texts: equal free credits, no purchase, no redemption, no prize taken from stakes, skill score if any prize exists. Inside: an entry fee; bought credits; credits exchanged for a prize or for a chance at a prize; a cut; a pool; a drawing; staking anything of value on a game, an election, a hackathon result, or any other contingent event. Skill does not cure a stake. Section 337a(a)(6) expressly covers the result of a contest of skill.

Sections 330b and 337j were not opened. They are named in Business and Professions Code section 17539.1(d) as laws that section does not repeal. They are not analyzed here.

The July 3, 2025 Attorney General opinion is the current official application of 337a to paid prediction-style games. It is discussed in section 3. Direct download of the PDF was later blocked; quotations are from the leginfo-style extract of `https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/opinions/pdfs/23-1001_1.pdf` returned in search, and from the Department of Justice announcement page.

## 2. Business and Professions Code 17539.1 (and 17539.3, 17539.35)

Official pages opened in full: [17539.1](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=17539.1.&lawCode=BPC), [17539.3](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17539.3), [17539.35](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17539.35), and [17539](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17539).

Section 17539 says the Legislature wanted "more complete disclosure of rules and operation of contests in which money or other valuable consideration may be solicited."

**When the disclosure article applies.** Section 17539.3(e) defines "contest," for sections 17539 through 17539.2, as a game or plan that offers a chance to receive or compete for gifts or prizes "as determined by skill or any combination of chance and skill and that is, or in whole or in part may be, conditioned upon the payment of consideration." A contest that never asks for payment is outside that definition. That is not permission to advertise vaguely. If any payment, sponsor purchase, or optional fee appears, the definition is met and the duties turn on.

**What must be disclosed, and what is forbidden, once 17539.1 applies.** From the text opened, a person operating a contest or sweepstakes must not:

- Fail to disclose, at the first solicitation and each time money is required, the total number of contestants anticipated and the percentages who solved each puzzle in the three most recent contests (subdivision (a)(1)).
- Misrepresent "the odds of winning any prize" (a)(3), or misrepresent the rules (a)(4).
- Fail to disclose, with the puzzles, the maximum number of rounds, the maximum amount of money a participant may be asked to pay, that later rounds will be significantly harder, the end date and the date prizes will be awarded, the method if a tie remains, and all rules (a)(5).
- Fail to disclose "the exact nature and approximate value of the prizes" (a)(6), or fail to award the prizes represented (a)(7).
- Pretend a particular person has won unless there was a real contest in which "at least a majority" of participants failed to win (a)(9)).
- Use "lucky" to imply an entry has an advantage others lack (a)(11)).
- Run the simulated-gambling systems described in (a)(12), discussed below.
- Fail, where an extra prize is offered for an optional payment, to say the payment is optional and to offer a free method "in immediate conjunction" with the paid method (a)(15)). The statute caps the postage-and-handling fee in that specific tie-breaker at one dollar and fifty cents plus actual postage. That is a mailing-fee cap inside (a)(15). It is not a registration or bonding threshold.

Section 17539.2, opened in part, adds duties for a person who "conducts any contest" as defined in 17539.3: deadline on the entry blank, refunds of consideration when the person cannot participate, a winners list on request, and two-year records. If Campus Call never takes consideration, this article's own definition does not pick the demo up. Publish rules anyway.

**Skill contest versus chance sweepstakes in this article.** Section 17539.1(b)(3) defines "sweepstakes" as a procedure for distribution of anything of value "by lot, chance, predetermined selection, or random selection" that is not already unlawful under Penal Code lottery and gaming chapters. Section 17539.3(e) defines "contest" to include prizes determined by skill or by mixed chance and skill, when consideration may be required. The code treats "who scores best" and "who is drawn" as different things. A Brier-score leaderboard is in the skill sentence. A drawing is in the sweepstakes sentence. Mixing them (skill points, then a drawing among the top ten) puts chance back in.

**The January 1, 2026 amendments.** The leginfo legend on 17539.1 says: amended by Stats. 2025, Ch. 623, Sec. 2 (AB 831), effective January 1, 2026. The hackathon is after that date. Subdivision (a)(12) prohibits using a website or app to simulate gambling, including what the statute calls "sports wagering," and games that mimic it, where the system predetermines prizes, connects a participant to a prize, or uses a dual-currency system of direct or indirect consideration and the person may become eligible for a prize or a chance at a prize. The same paragraph says it "does not make a game that does not award cash prizes or cash equivalents unlawful." It also says it does not make unlawful an occasional promotion incidental to real product sales and not meant as ongoing gambling. Subdivision (d) then says nothing in the section renders lawful any activity that is unlawful under Penal Code sections 320, 321, 322, 330, 330a, 330b, 330.1, 337a, or 337j. Read together: a no-cash-prize game is outside that one new paragraph; the paragraph does not bless a Penal Code violation; and purchased tokens, or tokens that can be turned in for a prize, are "consideration" by definition in (b)(1) and (b)(2).

**Registration and bonding.** Sections 17539.3 and 17539.35 were the only sections searched for a dollar registration or bonding trigger, as assigned. Neither section states one. Section 17539.3 is definitions, exclusions, and a statement that these sections do not permit a contest forbidden by other law. Section 17539.35 says: "No person shall advertise, offer, or operate any contest, as defined in subdivision (e) of Section 17539.3, in which any prize ... may be awarded ... if the opportunity to win that prize is conditioned on a minimum number of entries or contest participants." There is no dollar figure. Practical consequence: if the demo ever takes consideration, do not condition a prize on a minimum number of entrants. A free demo should not do that either if a prize is attached.

A related section, 17539.15, appeared in search (a clear no-purchase-or-payment-necessary statement on sweepstakes solicitations). The full leginfo page did not load. It is not relied on here. A lawyer should read it before any mailed or heavily designed sweepstakes solicitation.

Charitable organizations are carved out of the word "person" in 17539.3(b). A student hackathon team is not that carve-out unless it actually is a 501(c) or Revenue and Taxation Code 23701d entity. Do not assume the carve-out applies.

## 3. Skill contest versus chance under California law

**Statute.** Penal Code section 319, opened via leginfo: "A lottery is any scheme for the disposal or distribution of property by chance, among persons who have paid or promised to pay any valuable consideration for the chance of obtaining such property ... upon any agreement, understanding, or expectation that it is to be distributed or disposed of by lot or chance, whether called a lottery, raffle, or gift enterprise, or by whatever name the same may be known." Three elements are in that sentence: property, chance, and valuable consideration paid or promised for the chance. Remove consideration and the statutory lottery definition is not met. Remove chance, and it is not met either. A skill contest can still be illegal under section 337a if people stake value on the result. See section 1.

**Case opened.** *In re Allen* (1962) 59 Cal.2d 5, quoted from the Justia reproduction of the California Supreme Court opinion ([law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/59/5.html)), not from an official reporter PDF. The court said: "It is the character of the game rather than a particular player's skill or lack of it that determines whether the game is one of chance or skill. The test is not whether the game contains an element of chance or an element of skill but which of them is the dominating factor in determining the result of the game." The court held bridge predominantly a game of skill even though the deal involves chance, because decisions in the bidding and play ordinarily determine the outcome. It cited *People v. Settles*, 29 Cal.App.2d Supp. 781. Settles was not opened separately. Do not treat a secondary summary of Settles as a holding of this memo.

Allen is not a holding about forecasting. It is the dominating-factor test. Whether skill predominates is a question of fact. A leaderboard that ranks people by a pre-published accuracy score is built so the result is the quality of their forecasts. A drawing is built so the result is chance. No case opened here held that a forecasting contest, a prediction market, or a Brier score is predominantly skill.

**Attorney General, July 3, 2025.** Opinion No. 23-1001, requested by Assemblymember Tom Lackey. The Department of Justice announcement is at [oag.ca.gov](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/california-department-justice-releases-legal-opinion-daily-fantasy-sports). The opinion PDF is `https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/opinions/pdfs/23-1001_1.pdf`. A later direct fetch returned "Access Denied." The quotations below are from the search extract of that official PDF.

The question was whether California law prohibits daily fantasy sports games with players physically in California, even if the operator is elsewhere. The conclusion in the extract: "Yes, California law prohibits the operation of daily fantasy sports games with players physically located within California, regardless of where the operators and associated technology are located." "Such games constitute wagering on sports in violation of Penal Code section 337a."

On the paid pick'em format, the extract says players "try to win money by predicting" an athlete's performance in a real game, and concludes that format violates 337a "because the game's entry fees are 'bets' or 'wagers' placed 'upon the result ... of [a] trial ... or contest ... of skill.'" The Attorney General did not need to decide skill versus chance to reach that result.

On the lottery question, the extract says distribution by chance "means that winning and losing depend on luck and fortune rather than, or at least more than, judgment and skill," and that when a game combines both, the question is which is "the dominating factor in determining the result," looking to "the character of the game as revealed by its rules." It then says the office is "unable to opine as to whether daily fantasy games satisfy the distribution-by-chance requirement" because whether skill or chance predominates "is a question of fact," and "the answer might also vary by game." The extract quotes a commentator that it is difficult to predict what a court would find, and says resolving the lottery question is outside an Attorney General legal opinion.

Use of that opinion for Campus Call, without stretching it:

- Paid entry, used to win money, on the result of a real contest, was treated as a 337a violation for players in California. Forecasting skill was not a defense in that opinion.
- The opinion does not say a free, non-redeemable forecasting demo is illegal.
- The opinion does not say forecasting is a game of skill. It says that question is factual and was not decided.
- Secondary articles about a "50 percent" test were not used. Allen's phrase is "dominating factor." The Attorney General's phrase is the same idea. Neither source opened here states a numeric percentage as the legal test.

## 4. Free-to-play prizes: consideration, chance, and a prize

The classic three elements, in California's own words, are in Penal Code section 319: distribution of property, by chance, to people who paid or promised to pay valuable consideration for that chance. Federal consumer guidance lines up with the consideration point and does not replace the Penal Code.

FTC consumer guidance opened: ["Fake Prize, Sweepstakes, and Lottery Scams"](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/fake-prize-sweepstakes-and-lottery-scams) says "Real sweepstakes are free and by chance" and "It's illegal to ask you to pay or buy something to enter or to increase your odds of winning." The FTC's June 27, 2023 Publishers Clearing House release ([ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/ftc-takes-action-against-publishers-clearing-house-misleading-consumers-about-sweepstakes-entries)) charged dark patterns that made people believe a purchase was required to enter or would increase the chance of winning, and that an entry was incomplete when it was not. The proposed order would require a clear statement that a purchase is not required and will not help the person win, plus a path to enter with no sales message.

**What "no purchase necessary" and "free to enter" do.** They are how an operator tries to remove consideration. If the free method is real, equally available, and not buried, a chance promotion is missing the consideration element of a lottery. California's 17539.1(a)(15) is the same idea for one optional-payment phase: the payment must be disclosed as optional, and the free method must sit next to the paid method.

**What they do not do.**

- They do not legalize a stake of value on a contingent event under Penal Code 337a. Free entry into a pool that still stakes credits-with-value is not "no consideration."
- They do not fix a misleading rule, a fake "you won," or a purchase that is implied to help. That is 17539.1(a)(3), (a)(4), and (a)(9), and the FTC's PCH theory.
- They do not turn a drawing into a skill contest.
- They do not erase indirect consideration. Under 17539.1(b)(2), a token provided free is still indirect consideration if it "may be exchanged for a prize, award, cash, or cash equivalents or a chance to win" those things.
- They do not authorize a dual-currency system in which one currency is bought and the other is "free," if either currency can make a person eligible for a prize. See 17539.1(a)(12) and the definitions in (b).

**Skill prize versus a drawing.** A skill prize ranks every eligible forecaster by a rule published in advance. For this demo that rule should be the Brier score: the mean of the squared differences between the forecasted market probability and the outcome, coded 0 or 1. Lower is better. Points may be a simple transform of that score (for example, a fixed starting total minus the Brier score). The winner is the best score. Ties should split the prize or go to the earlier last-update time. A drawing, including a drawing to break a tie, is chance. Section 17539.1(a)(5)(E) expects the tie method to be disclosed. Do not disclose "random" as that method.

A skill prize can still be illegal if people paid consideration for a chance and chance in fact dominates, or if the "skill" label is sitting on top of stakes. The safe hackathon shape is: no consideration, and a score that is only accuracy.

## 5. CFTC event contracts, and four real products

Campus Call must stay play-money. It must not copy a real-money exchange, must not list contracts for money, and must not send users to one.

**Kalshi.** The CFTC's November 4, 2020 press release ([8302-20](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8302-20)) says the Commission issued an Order of Designation to KalshiEX LLC "granting it status as a designated contract market (DCM)" under section 5 of the Commodity Exchange Act and CFTC Regulation 38.3(a). The CFTC industry-filings page for Kalshi ([cftc.gov](https://www.cftc.gov/IndustryOversight/IndustryFilings/TradingOrganizations/42993)) lists status "Designated" and says that on January 17, 2025 the Commission granted a petition to modify the order to permit intermediated futures trading. Kalshi is a federally designated exchange. Its contracts are real derivatives. A student app is not a DCM and cannot "just do a small Kalshi."

**Polymarket.** The CFTC's January 3, 2022 press release ([8478-22](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8478-22)) says the Commission settled charges against Blockratize, Inc. d/b/a Polymarket for offering off-exchange event-based binary options and for failing to obtain designation as a DCM or registration as a swap execution facility. The order required a $1.4 million civil monetary penalty, a wind-down of noncompliant markets on Polymarket.com, and a cease-and-desist. The order found that from about June 2020 Polymarket had operated an unregistered facility for event markets, using smart contracts on a blockchain. The order PDF is linked from [cftc.gov/media/6891](https://www.cftc.gov/media/6891) (Docket No. 22-09).

Later status, only as opened: a CFTC industry-filings page ([49571](https://www.cftc.gov/IndustryOversight/IndustryFilings/TradingOrganizations/49571)) lists "QCX LLC d/b/a Polymarket US," status "Designated," date 2025-07-09, with the remark "QCX LLC is now operating under the assumed name of Polymarket US." A direct open of that index page failed on one attempt; the text above is from the search extract of that CFTC URL. A December 30, 2025 Regulation 40.6(a) letter on cftc.gov (`https://www.cftc.gov/filings/orgrules/rules12302535964.pdf`) describes "QCX LLC, d/b/a Polymarket US ... a designated contract market" self-certifying rulebook amendments. So the 2022 order is about an unregistered crypto event venue. The 2025–2026 filings are about a named DCM, QCX LLC, using the Polymarket US name. Those are different legal structures. Neither is a model for Campus Call. An unregistered event market was fined. A DCM is a federally designated exchange. A hackathon demo is neither, and should not pretend to be either.

**Manifold.** Its own terms, last updated January 31, 2026, were opened at [docs.manifold.markets/terms](https://docs.manifold.markets/terms). Manifold Markets, Inc. says Mana is play-money "for entertainment, learning, and non-monetary contest purposes," is not legal tender, a security, or a commodity, and "has no cash or redeemable value." The same paragraph says "While Mana may be purchased through the Site, it cannot be cashed out, sold, traded, transferred, gifted, or exchanged ... for money, property, products, services, or any other consideration." Section 6.8 says participation "does not constitute real-money gambling, investing, or financial trading." Section 6.5(5) says that because Mana has no monetary value, "it is not subject to — and the Site is not operated under — any financial-services or gambling regulations." That sentence is the company's assertion. It is not a CFTC order. Section 6.9 says prize drawings are governed by separate rules, that "No purchase is necessary to enter or win," and that a purchase will not improve the chance of winning. The terms also say the site is for users at least 13, and that minors need a parent or guardian to agree.

Do not copy Manifold. Purchasable credits plus a prize drawing are the facts California's 2026 definitions call direct consideration and a sweepstakes. Their "we are not regulated" sentence does not bind the Attorney General or the CFTC. Campus Call credits should not be for sale at all.

The public FAQ at [docs.manifold.markets/faq](https://docs.manifold.markets/faq) still says users "may choose to buy additional mana" and that it cannot be converted to cash. That matches the January 31, 2026 terms on the purchase point. An older about-page description of Mana as never purchasable is stale. Rely on the terms dated January 31, 2026.

**Metaculus.** The FAQ opened at [metaculus.com/faq](https://www.metaculus.com/faq/) says: "Metaculus has some similarities to a prediction market, but ultimately is not one." Markets, it says, generally use real or virtual currency where people buy or sell shares. "Metaculus, in contrast, directly solicits predicted probabilities from its users, then aggregates those probabilities." The same FAQ says questions should respect privacy, should not target the personal lives of non-public figures, should not predict the mortality of an individual or a small group, and should not be written to incentivize illegal or harmful acts. The terms opened at [metaculus.com/terms-of-use](https://www.metaculus.com/terms-of-use) (last modified September 21, 2026) describe a platform "to predict future events" and display a track record, and say Metaculus may decide "how much value (points or other value) to assign or take away from your profile, which is public." "Other value" is loose. Nothing opened on that page describes Metaculus as a DCM or as a place that takes stakes of money. The closer public model for Campus Call is this one: a person states a probability, the site scores it, and points are not cashed. Do not add a share-trading layer on top.

**2024–2026 CFTC status that was actually opened.**

- February 4, 2026, press release [9179-26](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9179-26): the Commission withdrew the notice of proposed rulemaking titled "Event Contracts" that was published June 10, 2024, and said it does not intend to issue final rules on that proposal. Staff also withdrew a September 30, 2025 advisory on sports event contracts. The withdrawal is not a statement that an unregistered app may offer event contracts.
- February 17, 2026, press release [9183-26](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9183-26): the Commission filed an amicus brief in the Ninth Circuit in *North American Derivatives Exchange, Inc. v. Nevada* "confirming its exclusive jurisdiction" over commodity derivatives markets, "including event contract markets." The release says states do not have authority to regulate markets inside that exclusive jurisdiction. It also says the CFTC "first officially recognized event contracts in 1992 when it allowed the Iowa Electronic Markets." That history is about a federally allowed university futures market, not about a hackathon website. Campus Call has no such allowance. The same release is evidence that states and DCMs are in active litigation. It is not a holding that California Penal Code section 337a no longer applies to a non-DCM product with players in California. The Attorney General's 2025 opinion, above, says the opposite for paid daily fantasy games.

A student team cannot sit in the middle of that fight by shipping a real-money contract. Play-money, no purchase, no redemption.

## 6. CSU and Cal Poly student conduct

Pages opened:

## 7. FERPA and academic markets

FERPA is the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act. The definition that matters is in [34 CFR 99.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/subtitle-A/part-99/subpart-A/section-99.3) (eCFR; the eCFR's own banner says it is not the official legal edition of the CFR). "Personally identifiable information" includes the student's name and identifiers, and also:

"Other information that, alone or in combination, is linked or linkable to a specific student that would allow a reasonable person in the school community, who does not have personal knowledge of the relevant circumstances, to identify the student with reasonable certainty."

It also includes information requested by someone the school reasonably believes already knows who the student is.

The Department of Education's disclosure-avoidance FAQ ([studentprivacy.ed.gov PDF](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/FAQs_disclosure_avoidance_0.pdf), the text returned in search) says the de-identification standard asks whether that reasonable person could identify a student from the release plus other reasonably available information, "including other public information released by an agency, such as a report presenting detailed data in tables with small size cells," citing 34 CFR 99.3 and 99.31(b)(1). The same FAQ says reporting a small cell "does not by itself constitute a disclosure," but "the smaller the cell size, the greater the likelihood" of identification. "Many statisticians consider a cell size of 3 to be the absolute minimum needed to prevent disclosure, though larger minimums (e.g., 5 or 10) may be used." It also says school officials are not the "reasonable person," because they are presumed to know the students. A separate ED FAQ ([studentprivacy.ed.gov](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/when-organization-publishes-results-study-can-it-publish-results-way-allows-individual-students)) says that, absent prior written consent, FERPA prohibits publishing personally identifiable information from education records "in a way that would allow individual students and their parents to be identified," and that tables may need cell suppression.

Directory information, in the 99.3 list opened on eCFR, includes name, major, dates of attendance, degrees, and similar items "that would not generally be considered harmful or an invasion of privacy if disclosed." Grades, exam scores, and class averages are not on that list.

**When a "class average" becomes identifiable.** There is no Cal Poly number in the sources opened, and ED does not set a single required cell size. A class average is identifiable when a reasonable person in the campus community, without inside knowledge, could tie it to a student with reasonable certainty. That happens when the section is small, when one student is an outlier (the average moves when everyone knows who failed), when the average is crossed with a roster, a discussion post, or another release, or when a suppressed cell can be backed out of a published total. The FAQ's own caution is that suppressing the small cell may not be enough. For a hackathon, the workable rule is: no market on a class average at all.

**Why these markets are out.**

- A single student's grade, pass, or rank is that student's education record, or a solicitation to disclose it. Consent would have to be written and informed. A public market is the opposite of that.
- A small section fails the reasonable-person test as soon as classmates can see who is who. ED's "3 / 5 / 10" discussion is a risk band for statisticians, not a clearance to forecast a 30-person lab.
- An unpublished exam is an education record and an academic-integrity problem. A market on the exam's contents or results pressures people to leak it. Title 5 section 41301(b)(1)(A), on the OSRR page, treats cheating and academic dishonesty intended to gain an unfair academic advantage as a conduct violation. Building a points game on a live exam is that fact pattern.

Also out, by the Metaculus FAQ's own public standards and by ordinary privacy: a named non-public student's life, health, or discipline case. Campus Call is not a school official and has no FERPA exception to receive education records.

## 8. Age

California Family Code section 6500, opened on leginfo: "A minor is an individual who is under 18 years of age." The Division 11 display on leginfo states the next section: "An adult is an individual who is 18 years of age or older." Section 6502, on that same display, says that on or after March 4, 1972, a reference to the age of majority means 18. So the age of majority in California is 18. That statute does not say "forecasting contests must be 18-plus." It says who is a minor.

**Can the hackathon include minors?** Not verified for this event. [events.calpoly.edu/event-listing](https://events.calpoly.edu/event-listing) lists "Cal Poly AI Hackathon," Friday, November 13, 2026 through Sunday, November 15, 2026, 12:00pm. The guessed detail URL `https://events.calpoly.edu/event/cal-poly-ai-hackathon` returned "Page not found." No eligibility age was on the listing snippet. Cal Poly undergraduates can be 17. A campus event can also have guests. A different program, the CSU AI Summer Camp 2026 call for applicants posted by the College of Engineering ([PDF](https://ceng.calpoly.edu/sites/default/files/news-documents/Call-for-Applicants_CSU_26-1.pdf)), says applicants must be current CSU students and "Must be over the age of 18 by July 12th." That is a different event. It shows that at least one Cal Poly-hosted AI program required 18. It does not prove this hackathon does.

**Recommendation.** If the demo has any prize with real value, limit eligibility to people who are 18 or older. Reason: they are adults under Family Code 6500 and 6501; a minor's right to receive a prize, sign a release, or consent to use of a name is a lawyer question; and this memo could not verify that the November hackathon excludes minors. The recommendation is not itself a statute. A points-only demo with no prize can still use 18-plus as a simple rule and avoid the question. Manifold's terms allow 13-year-olds with a parent's agreement and then run separate prize drawings. That split is their product, not a model.

## Official Rules draft

Draft only. Not approved by a lawyer or by the hackathon. Do not post it as official until both have signed off. Replace the bracketed lines.

**Campus Call Official Rules — free forecasting contest**

1. **Sponsor.** Campus Call is a student demo at the Cal Poly AI Hackathon, November 13–15, 2026. It is not operated by California Polytechnic State University, the California State University, or the hackathon, unless a written line is added here: [organizer name, if any]. Contact: [name, email].

2. **No purchase necessary.** The contest is free. A purchase will not help a score, will not grant credits, and will not improve a person's chance at a prize. There is no paid path.

3. **What it is.** A person forecasts how likely a stated outcome is by taking a position at the current market probability. The demo shows that probability and awards points for accuracy. Virtual credits are only a counter inside the demo.

4. **Credits.** Each eligible person receives the same number of virtual credits at the start. Credits have no cash value. They cannot be bought, sold, transferred, gifted, or redeemed for money, goods, or a prize. They are a limited license. The operators may reset them when the demo ends. They are not the person's property.

5. **How a winner is picked.** If a prize is offered, the winner is the eligible person with the best forecasting score at [end time and time zone]. The score is the Brier score: the average, across that person's scored forecasts, of the squared difference between the market probability they forecast and the outcome (1 if the outcome happened, 0 if it did not). A lower Brier score is better. Points equal [starting points] minus that score, so more points are better. The published leaderboard is the record. This is a skill ranking. It is not a drawing. If two people tie, they split the prize equally. If a prize cannot be split, the person whose last forecast update was earlier wins. No chance device is used.

6. **Prize.** [Describe the prize and its approximate cash value, or write "No prize. Points and rank only."] If no prize is described here, none exists. Operators will award the prize described and no other. A stated prize that is not awarded will be disclosed to entrants.

7. **Eligibility.** Open to persons who are 18 years of age or older at the start of the contest and who are [hackathon participants / others]. Void where prohibited. People who are not eligible may not receive a prize. The operators may close an account that breaks these rules. Points from that account do not count.

8. **Who may not forecast an event.** A person may not take a position on an event if they are an employee, judge, or organizer of that event, or if they are on a team whose own outcome is the subject of the market. A team may not forecast its own project, placement, or award. Positions taken in violation of this section are void and do not earn points.

9. **Topics that will not be offered.** No market on a student's grade or academic standing, a class average, a small course section, an unpublished exam or assignment, a person's health, or a question written so that a harmful or illegal act would decide the result. No market that uses the University's name, the hackathon's name, or a sponsor's name, unless that use is approved in writing and added to these rules.

10. **Resolutions.** Operators will publish how each question resolves before it opens. Their good-faith application of that written standard is final for the demo. A question that cannot be resolved as written is void. Void questions do not change the score.

11. **Data.** The demo will not ask for a student ID number, a grade, or an education record. Display names may be shown on the leaderboard. A person may use a pseudonym.

12. **End.** The contest ends at the time in section 5, or earlier if the operators post a cancellation. Cancellation is allowed if the demo breaks these rules or if the venue requires it. If a prize was promised and the contest is cancelled before a winner is picked, the prize is not awarded by chance; it is not awarded, and that fact will be posted.

13. **Law.** These rules are governed by the law of the State of California, except where federal law controls. They do not authorize any stake of money or anything of value.

## Top 5 takeaways

1. The safe hackathon shape is equal free credits, no purchase, no redemption, no cash value, and, if any prize exists, a published accuracy score. An entry fee, bought credits, or credits that convert into a prize are the facts that move the demo toward Penal Code 337a and the 2026 sweepstakes definitions.
2. A skill label does not legalize a stake. The Attorney General's July 3, 2025 opinion treated paid fantasy predictions by people in California as violations of section 337a, and refused to decide whether skill or chance predominates. *In re Allen* supplies the dominating-factor test and does not decide this product.
3. Do not copy Kalshi or Polymarket. One is a designated contract market. The other was fined in 2022 for unregistered event markets and, in 2025–2026 filings, a different entity using the Polymarket US name is a DCM. Metaculus (state a probability, get a score) is the closer public model. Manifold sells play credits and runs separate prize drawings; that is not the model.
4. Title 5, as posted by OSRR, does not name gambling. Dishonesty, pretending to speak for the University, alcohol, and a criminal violation that threatens the campus still can. The posted 2021–22 athlete handbook forbids sports stakes and sharing team information. FERPA makes a market on one student, a small class, a class average, or an unpublished exam a privacy and conduct problem.
5. Organizer approval comes before any market on the hackathon, any use of the event name, any Discord prize post, or any sponsor prize. A lawyer comes before any prize of real value, any real-money referral, any purchasable credit, or any launch beyond the room. This memo is not either approval.
